# M1: A world to explore

The work items of [M1](../ROADMAP.md#m1-a-world-to-explore), in order, and the
decisions they share. The roadmap keeps the goal and exit criterion. This
spec is deleted when M1 ends; [Afterwards](#afterwards) lists what moves where.

M1 builds the mechanisms, not complete content. The biome table, the generator
and the set of logical asset IDs keep changing after M1.

## Shared decisions

### The surface and its parameters

M1 generates one space, the [surface](../CONTEXT.md#spaces-and-terrain). Other
spaces, such as dungeons or other worlds, may later be generated too, each
with its own parameters. Nothing in the terrain format assumes that a space
has a latitude or is the surface.

**The surface is a band of latitude**, as [the
vision](../../docs/VISION.md#decisions-so-far) describes. Each row has a
latitude, interpolated between the latitudes of the north and south edges. A
band may lie in one hemisphere or cross the equator. Seasons need each row's
latitude at runtime (see [Climate and seasons](#climate-and-seasons)), so the
terrain of a space with a latitude stores it as data.

**Generation is configured, never hard-coded.** The player will eventually
choose the generation parameters when a game starts, as advanced settings. M1
does not build those settings, but the generator is ready for them:

- Every value the generator uses is a parameter, in a struct with no defaults
  that the game binary supplies. The struct describes one generated space,
  not the world. For the surface, it includes the width and height in chunks,
  the latitudes of the north and south edges, and the share of tiles under
  water.
- Parameters use units that a player understands, such as degrees of latitude,
  degrees Celsius and shares of the surface. The generator converts them.
- An invalid combination of parameters returns an error that names the
  problem. It never panics.

**One seed per game.** Generation derives a stream of randomness for each
space from the game's seed and a stable label for that space, as the
[reproducibility invariant](../INVARIANTS.md#reproducibility) requires.

**The surface is generated once, completely, at genesis.** The surface is
finite, so the generator builds all of it before the first tick, not chunk by
chunk on demand. Parallel generation must give the same result as sequential
generation.

After genesis, the terrain is data. No code recomputes it from the seed or
samples the noise again to find out what a tile holds. A future save therefore
stores the terrain, and stays valid when the generator changes. The terrain
keeps the seed and the generation parameters only to record where it came
from. What the simulation or presentation needs at runtime, such as each row's
latitude, is terrain data and is never read from the parameters. A tile stores
the index of its biome in the table; a save will store the stable name, so
that changing the table never breaks a save. Presentation may still derive
purely visual variety, such as which grass sprite a tile shows, from its
position.

**Presentation shares the terrain without copying it.** The world keeps each
chunk behind a shared, immutable pointer, and the snapshot carries the set of
pointers. When farming or building later change a chunk, only that chunk is
replaced (copy on write). A snapshot's cost therefore does not grow with the
terrain, and presentation never sees a half-changed chunk.

### Generation

**Generation runs in passes.** A pass is one step over the whole space. It reads
what earlier passes wrote and writes one new layer. The passes of v0, in order:

1. **Elevation:** seamless noise.
2. **Temperature:** the winter and summer temperatures (see [Climate and
   seasons](#climate-and-seasons)).
3. **Moisture:** seamless noise. A later pass may derive moisture from physical
   causes, such as the distance to water.
4. **Vegetation:** seamless noise, at a large scale.
5. **Biome:** each tile's biome, from its fields and the biome table.
6. **Biome clean-up:** removes biome regions below a minimum size.
7. **Obstacles:** places the obstacles of each biome.

A later feature, such as rivers, erosion or oceanicity, is a new pass in this
list.

**The noise is seamless and written in-house.** It samples a cylinder (`x`
becomes an angle), so that no seam shows. Each field has its own scale and its
own stream of randomness from the seed.

**A field pass may smooth its result.** Each field pass has a smoothing radius,
which can be 0. Smoothing wraps across the seam. It matters most for
temperature, which would otherwise copy the sharp features of elevation.

**Generation stays easy to change.** It will see many iterations, so:

- The generator is its own crate, `worldgen`. The rest of the game sees only
  the terrain. The passes and their intermediate data, such as unquantised
  values, stay private to the generator.
- Adding, replacing or reordering a pass changes only the generator.
- Its tests check properties only: no golden maps, no expected biome
  proportions, no values at a fixed seed. Code that reads terrain is tested on
  small hand-built terrain and never calls the generator.

### Climate and seasons

**A tile's climate is two temperatures:** its winter temperature and its
summer temperature, the coldest and the warmest average of the year, not
extremes. Their mean is the annual mean, and their difference is the
seasonal swing. Biomes can then use the same thresholds as real climate
classifications, such as "winter below −10 °C".

**The temperature pass combines:**

- **Latitude.** The annual mean falls from the equator to the poles, and the
  swing grows. The mean and the swing at the equator and at the poles are
  parameters.
- **Altitude.** A cooling that grows with elevation above sea level lowers
  both temperatures. The cooling at the highest tile is a parameter.
- **Continentality**, a factor on the swing. In v0 it is 1 on every tile. A
  later oceanicity pass, after elevation, computes it from the distance to the
  sea, wrapping across the seam: the swing narrows near the coast and widens
  inland. That pass then only supplies the factor.
- **Noise**, whose strength is a parameter.

**The calendar gives a date, and each tile has its own season.** The calendar
season is a quarter of the year, named for display. A tile's season is the
calendar season, shifted by half a year south of the equator. One function
computes a tile's temperature at a date, from its winter and summer
temperatures, its latitude and the date. It follows a smooth yearly cycle that
peaks in the middle of the tile's summer. No other code combines climate with
the date, and a later daily cycle or seasonal lag goes into that function.
Nothing stores the current temperature: it is computed when needed, as the
[invariants](../INVARIANTS.md#simulation-steps-tolerate-variable-elapsed-time)
require.

### Fields

**A field is quantised** to a `u16`. Integers keep tiles and future saves
small, and the terrain hashable and exactly comparable, which floats are not.
Every `u16` is a valid value.

**Each field has the scale that keeps it predictable:**

| Field | Scale | What a threshold means |
|---|---|---|
| Elevation | Rank in the space, from −1 (deepest) through 0 (sea level) to 1 (highest) | Below 0, a share of the water tiles; above 0, a share of the land tiles. |
| Winter and summer temperature | The temperature scale, in degrees Celsius | The same climate in every space, whatever its latitudes. |
| Moisture | Rank among land tiles | A share of the land tiles. |
| Vegetation | Rank among land tiles | A share of the land tiles. |

A ranked field is spread evenly over its range: the share of tiles below a
value is that value's share of the range. Smoothing comes before ranking, so it
does not change the spread. A rank describes this space only.

Elevation is ranked separately below and above sea level, and moisture and
vegetation are ranked among land tiles, with water tiles ranked among
themselves. A threshold of the biome table therefore keeps its meaning when
the share under water changes, and when a later pass makes moisture depend on
the distance to water.

Temperature is not ranked, because a band in temperate latitudes alone must
not get the coldest biome in its coldest rows. **The temperature scale** is a
constant of the terrain format, in the `terrain` crate next to the fields,
with the conversions between degrees Celsius and `u16`. It is wide enough,
such as −150 °C to +150 °C in steps of about 0.005 °C, that no space needs
more, so it is never tuned. Generation clamps values outside it, and rejects
temperature parameters outside it. Changing the scale changes the terrain
format.

### Biomes

**Biomes are content.** The engine never names a biome. The game binary
supplies the biome table, as it supplies the input bindings. The table moves to
content files when the `content` crate exists.

**The biome table lists biomes in order.** Each biome has:

- a stable name;
- a range for each field; a field without a range accepts any value;
- a map colour;
- whether it is impassable, as water is;
- its obstacle kinds, each with a density and an optional variation (see
  [obstacles](#obstacles));
- later, its logical asset IDs.

Ranges are written in the field's unit, as the [Fields](#fields) table gives
it, and converted when the table is loaded: −1 to 1 for elevation, degrees
Celsius for the temperatures, a share of land for moisture and vegetation.

The biome table is shared by every space. Later, another kind of space may
choose biomes with its own ordered ranges, over biome definitions that all
spaces share. A tile stores a biome index and a save its name, so that split
changes only how content is loaded.

**A tile takes the first biome whose ranges all contain its fields.** The order
gives priority: water first, below the sea level, then high ground, then the
biomes of each climate. The last biome has no ranges, so that every tile gets
a biome. Loading a table whose last biome has ranges fails.

Vegetation separates biomes that share a climate, such as a wooded biome and an
open one. A wooded area is therefore a place in the terrain, with a name and
borders, which perception, danger and wildlife can later refer to.

**Clean-up keeps biomes coherent.** A region is a set of tiles of one biome,
joined by their edges, across the seam too. Clean-up gives each region smaller
than the minimum size the biome most common along its border, the first in
table order on a tie. Clean-up repeats until no region is smaller than the
minimum size. The minimum size is one parameter for all biomes. After clean-up,
a tile's biome may differ from the one its fields select.

**An impassable biome blocks movement without obstacles.** Generation records
in each tile whether its biome is impassable, so that code reading the terrain
never needs the table.

### Obstacles

**Obstacles are a tile layer, with footprints.** In M1, an obstacle (a rock, a
tree, a fallen log) is part of the terrain, not an entity. Its footprint, the
tiles it blocks, is content: offsets from its anchor tile, one tile by default.
The anchor tile holds the obstacle, and each other tile of the footprint holds
the offset to it. A footprint may cross chunk boundaries and the seam, never the
north or south edge. It is unrelated to the sprite: a tree covers about 3×3
tiles but blocks only its trunk. The simulation never reads a sprite's size, so
swapping placeholders for the pack blocks no other tile. Whether obstacles
become entities, as buildings will, is decided in M2 or M3; footprints and
placement carry over either way.

**Obstacle kinds are content.** Each has a name and a footprint. The game
binary supplies the list with the biome table.

**Density is the chance that a tile anchors an obstacle of that kind.** A
density of 0.05 places about one obstacle per 20 tiles of the biome.

**Variation groups obstacles.** An obstacle kind of a biome may have a
variation: its own smooth noise, with a scale and a strength, which raises the
density in some places and lowers it in others. The average density stays the
one in the table: loading rejects a variation that could take the density
below 0 or above 1. Variation gives groves in open land, clearings in woods and
clusters of rocks. Each tile's chance depends only on the seed, its position
and the obstacle kind, never on obstacles already placed.

**Placement visits the tiles in a fixed order.** For each obstacle kind of the
tile's biome, a draw from randomness derived from the seed, the tile and the
obstacle kind decides whether the tile is an anchor. The obstacle is placed
only if every tile of its footprint is free and has a biome that lists that
obstacle kind. Otherwise it is skipped, so the density is an upper bound.

### Assets

**Placeholders cover all content; the pack covers part of it.** Every logical
asset ID has a placeholder, so content is never limited to what the pack can
show. The pack covers a subset of the IDs, and that subset changes when its
author or we add art. An ID without art, such as the ground of a desert biome,
shows its placeholder. Placeholders follow the pack's format: blob blocks in
its 12×4 layout, seasonal variants, animation frames and sprite proportions.
Swapping in the pack therefore needs no code change. Placeholders do not follow
the pack's content. They must be
[readable enough to play with](../../docs/VISION.md#decisions-so-far), across
the whole set, including what the pack does not show.

**Placeholders tell every biome apart; the final art need not.** Distinct
placeholders make generation easy to judge. The aim of the final art is a
varied world, not sharply different biomes: two biomes of one climate, such as
a forest and a meadow, may share their ground and differ mostly by their
obstacles.

## Work items

Each item is one pull request, or two if it grows.

### 1. Survey the asset pack

**Done.** The answers are in [the findings note](../findings/asset-pack.md).

### 2. World shape

**Done.** Wrapping is `Grid` in `core`. Chunks of 32×32 tiles, footprints and
shared terrain are in the `terrain` crate. A world's size is configured in
chunks, so it is always a whole number of them. Where the width is even, two
columns half a world apart are equally far both ways; the offset then goes
west whichever tile comes first. This item also took on interpolation across
the seam and keeping the wanderer off obstacles.

Until generation exists, a stand-in pattern in `sim` fills the terrain: fields
that follow the coordinates, biomes in latitude bands and obstacles on a
lattice. It means nothing. It is unrelated to the placeholder assets.

### 3. Generation v0

**Done.** The `worldgen` crate generates the surface in the passes above,
from `SurfaceParams` and the biome table, which are in the `terrain` crate
with the fields, their units and `temperature_at`. The game binary supplies
the default table and parameters, and the headless runner its own small
ones. `World::new` takes the generated surface, so code that reads terrain
is tested on hand-built terrain. The wanderer starts in the largest open
region, and the camera opens on it.

The open questions were settled as follows:

- **Size:** 64 × 32 chunks (2,048 × 1,024 tiles). Generation takes about
  0.17 s on 16 threads, and gives the same result on any number of threads.
  The passes are parallel, except the parts of biome clean-up and obstacle
  placement that depend on order.
- **Curves:** as recommended, the annual mean follows the cosine of the
  latitude and the swing its sine. Tuning is left to item 4.
- **Platforms:** a seed gives the same terrain on every platform. Sine and
  cosine come from `libm`, and the noise uses only operations that IEEE 754
  rounds exactly.

For item 6: the calendar year starts with spring, so the middle of the
northern summer is at 3/8 of the year.

### 4. Seeing the world

**Done.** The renderer colours each visible tile by biome or, in a debug
view, by one field, and the obstacle layer shades every footprint tile and
marks anchors with a light square. The camera wraps east–west and keeps its
centre canonical; a view across the seam, or wider than the surface, draws
every side with its entities. The debug overlay holds the tile inspector, the
views, the obstacle layer and regeneration. A `Genesis` in `app` creates
the world from the seed, the surface parameters and the biome table, and
regeneration restarts the simulation thread on a new world.

The open points were settled as follows:

- **Blocked tiles:** the obstacle layer marks obstacles only. Water and
  peaks show by their colour.
- **Regeneration** keeps the camera where it is, so that settings can be
  compared at one place. It blocks the window while it generates, about
  0.2 s.
- **Tuning** the default table is deferred: it is done step by step, once
  there is gameplay to tune it for.
- **Cost:** the instances are uploaded each frame, which is heavy at the
  smallest zoom on a large screen. Item 5 reworks the drawing.

### 5. Asset pipeline

Three pull requests: 5a cooks the placeholders, 5b draws the terrain from the
atlas, 5c draws the sprites. The decisions they share come first.

**Logical asset IDs come in two kinds**, terrains and sprites, each its own
type and namespace. A name is in snake_case and unique within its kind.
Loading resolves each name to a small index, so drawing never handles strings.
Seasonal variants and animation frames belong to the region an ID maps to;
they are not IDs.

- A biome names its ground terrain. The ground terrains form an ordered list
  in the content, the **drawing order**, from the lowest, such as deep water,
  to the highest. It is unrelated to the biome table's priority. Biomes may
  share a ground, and then show no edge between them.
- An obstacle kind names its sprite.
- The game binary gives the wanderer a stand-in sprite ID, until [actors
  replace it](../ROADMAP.md#m2-bodies-perception-and-movement).

**Blob tiles follow the pack's order.** The slot of each 8-neighbour mask in a
12×4 block is the table in [the findings note](../findings/asset-pack.md#tiles),
written once in code. Placeholders use it, so the pack's blocks drop in.

**A tile draws its layers** from the bottom: the lowest ground in its 3×3
neighbourhood as a full tile, then each higher ground present in the
neighbourhood, up to its own, as a blob tile. A layer's mask counts the
neighbours whose ground is at that layer or above it in the drawing order.

- A transition is drawn on the higher tile, as in the pack. A tile's own
  ground is never below a layer it draws, so the 47 shapes suffice.
- Neighbours wrap across the seam. Beyond the north or south edge, a neighbour
  counts as present, so no shore lines the edge of the world.
- A layer's blob block may depend on the layer just beneath it on that tile.
  The asset side may give a pair, such as grass over shallow water, its own
  block; otherwise the ground's own block is used. A pair is not an ID, and
  content never names one.

**Cooking is offline.** The game always loads a cooked container from disk,
whatever its art came from, so a clone without the pack runs the path of a
shipped game.

- `cook` is a library and a binary. Each source of art becomes the same
  source assets in memory: images per frame and seasonal variant, with a blob
  block, or a sprite rectangle and its anchor. Packing, padding, mipmaps,
  validation and writing are shared from there. Item 5 has one source, the
  placeholder recipes, which are input to `cook`, not to the game.
- The container is a format of our own: a header with a format version, the
  mapping from IDs to regions, and the atlases as raw RGBA. Its types are in
  the `cook` library, which `render` reads. Loading refuses another format
  version and says to cook again.
- `just cook` writes the container to the git-ignored `cooked/` folder, and
  `just game` runs it first. The game loads from a default path there,
  relative to the working directory, or from `--assets`, and cannot tell what
  art the container holds. A game started without cooking after a recipe
  changed shows the old art. Where a shipped game keeps its container is
  decided with packaging.

**Drawing.**

- Terrain instances are cached on the GPU per chunk, built when the chunk
  first shows and evicted least recently used within a budget. A cache entry
  stays valid while its chunk and the 8 around it are the same shared
  pointers, so replacing a chunk invalidates exactly what it affects. An
  instance holds the base region and the shader picks the seasonal variant
  (item 6), so a change of season rebuilds nothing.
- Obstacle sprites and entities are gathered and sorted each frame.
- Art pixels stay crisp at any zoom. The shader blends only across the screen
  pixel where two art pixels meet; the atlas has one mipmap level for zooms
  below 1×; each region is padded by repeating its edge pixels; the camera
  snaps to whole screen pixels. The smallest zoom, 0.5×, is for development:
  the game's will be larger.
- The field views keep their flat colours.

#### 5a. Cooked placeholders

- **Logical asset IDs** in the content, as above.
- **Placeholder recipes.** Each logical ID has a recipe, with one colour, or
  one colour per season. `cook` draws each seasonal variant as its own region.
  The rest of the recipe depends on the kind of asset:
  - A terrain has a pattern from a small fixed set, such as solid, stipple,
    hatching or waves. From it, `cook` draws the 47 blob tiles in the pack's
    12×4 layout. An animated terrain also has a frame count, and `cook` shifts
    the pattern from one frame to the next.
  - A sprite has a shape from a small fixed set, such as a canopy over a
    trunk, a boulder or a long capsule, drawn with an outline and a ground
    shadow. It also has a size and an anchor. A tall sprite with a pack
    equivalent follows its proportions (about 3×3 tiles for a large tree), so
    that occlusion and culling behave as they will with the pack. A flat
    sprite, such as a fallen log, matches its obstacle's footprint.

  Patterns are decoration; they also show the edges and the animation. Water
  and peaks look unlike any passable ground. Pattern features are at least 2
  pixels wide, so that they survive the mipmap.
- **`cook`** builds the container: atlases, and the mapping from logical IDs
  to regions. A terrain maps to a blob block, a sprite to a rectangle and an
  anchor. Each region has a frame list and seasonal variants. `cook` also
  computes the largest overhang of any sprite beyond its anchor tile.
- **`just cook`**, writing and loading the container.
- **Validation at load.** Loading fails, naming them, if content names
  logical IDs that the container lacks.
- **A view of the atlas** in the debug overlay. The renderer still draws flat
  colours.

**Tests:** over all 256 neighbour masks, a corner counts only when both of its
edges are present, giving 47 shapes, each in exactly one slot of the table;
packed regions, with their padding, lie inside the atlas and never overlap; a
container written and loaded back is unchanged; loading refuses another format
version; a missing logical ID fails validation.

#### 5b. Textured terrain

The textured pipeline, the layers with their masks and pairs, the per-chunk
cache and crisp pixels. Animated tiles show their first frame in M1.

**Tests**, on hand-built terrain: a mask at the seam uses the wrapped
neighbours, and one at the north or south edge counts the missing neighbours
as present; a tile draws exactly the layers that the rule gives; a pair's block
is used only over its lower ground; replacing a chunk invalidates its cache
entry and its neighbours'.

#### 5c. Sprites

Obstacle sprites, often larger than a tile, are drawn once, at their anchor,
in order of `y` with the entities, then of `x` and a stable key. Culling keeps
a sprite anchored outside the view whose image reaches into it, using the
largest overhang. Fading a canopy when something passes behind it waits for
the player character.

**Tests:** a sprite anchored outside the view but reaching into it is drawn;
an entity south of an obstacle's anchor is drawn after it, and one north of it
before.

#### When item 5 is done

The game runs with the placeholders alone and, at every zoom
level and without the inspector, you can tell apart:

- each biome of the default table from the others;
- passable tiles from impassable ones;
- where each obstacle blocks: its placeholder rests on its footprint, so its
  base covers the tiles it blocks, and only parts drawn above the ground, such
  as a canopy, reach beyond them;
- the entities from the terrain.

### 6. Calendar and seasons

- **Calendar in `core`**: the date, meaning the day, time of day, calendar
  season, day of the season and day of the year, from the tick and the
  configured lengths of a day and a season. The calendar knows nothing of
  latitude. Its date replaces the stand-in `TimeOfYear` that
  `temperature_at` takes.
- **The overlay** shows the date. The tile inspector adds the tile's season
  and its temperature at the current date.
- **Seasonal variants.** The renderer chooses each tile's variant from that
  tile's season at the snapshot's tick, as described in [Climate and
  seasons](#climate-and-seasons), and each sprite's from its anchor tile. An
  asset without variants never changes. Where a tile's swing is below a
  threshold, it shows one fixed variant instead, so that the tropics stay
  evergreen and no line appears at the equator. The content sets the threshold
  and the variant. In M1 the season affects only presentation. The
  placeholders of the default content have seasonal variants, so that a fresh
  clone also shows the seasons change.

**Tests:** values worked out by hand at the boundaries: the last tick of a day,
the first tick of a season, the first tick of a year. A tile south of the
equator is in the opposite season to a tile north of it, and a tile whose
swing is below the threshold always shows the fixed variant.

**Open:** how to watch the seasons change, since a season takes hours at 1×.
Recommended: a command-line option for the day length, for very short days in
development runs.

### 7. The real pack, locally

- **Configuration** locates the pack and our own art: our modifications of the
  pack and our additions in its style. Both stay outside the repository.
- **The provenance manifest** is committed: source, download date, checksum and
  licence. The pack has no version number, so the checksum covers every file.
  `cook` refuses a pack whose checksum differs.
- **The region mapping** is committed (it holds no image data). `cook` reads
  the pack in place through it, as another source of source assets (see item
  5); the pack's own organisation goes no further. Whether our own art is
  mapped too, or follows a naming convention, is decided here.
- **Fallback.** `cook` resolves each logical ID from our own art first, then
  from the pack, then from the placeholders. An ID takes all its art from one
  source. `cook` lists the IDs that fall back to a placeholder; a fallback is
  not an error. There is therefore one container, which holds every ID that
  has a placeholder: `just cook` cooks from the configured sources, and the
  game loads the result as in item 5.
- **Credit** to the author in the README, as the licence requires.
- CI never needs the pack.

**Tests**, on small hand-built inputs, without the pack: an ID that our own
art and the pack both lack resolves to its placeholder; our own art takes
precedence over the pack.

**Done when** the game runs locally with the pack, with transitions and
seasonal variants, and shows the placeholder of each logical ID that has no
art. It still runs from a fresh clone with the placeholders alone.
